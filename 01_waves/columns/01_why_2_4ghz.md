# Column 1: Why 2.4 GHz? — Wi-Fi's noisy neighborhood

Radio waves of different frequencies are like lanes on a very wide road. Countries
decide who may use which lane, so that TV, phones, airplanes and emergency radios do
not get in each other's way. Most lanes need a license.

## A lane anyone may use

Around **2.4 GHz** there is a special band that many countries leave open for
everyday devices, as long as they follow the rules (for example, not sending too
strongly). It was first set aside for "industrial, scientific and medical" machines, so
it is often called an **ISM band**.

Because anyone may use it, it is crowded. At home, these may all be using the 2.4 GHz
band at the same time:

| Device | Why it is there |
|---|---|
| Wi-Fi | connecting to the internet |
| Bluetooth | earphones, game controllers, smart watches |
| Microwave oven | it heats food with radio waves at about 2.45 GHz, and a little can leak out |
| Some cordless devices | toys, remote controls and so on |

Have you noticed Wi-Fi getting slow while the microwave oven is running? That can be
this neighborhood noise.

## Why this matters for our experiments

- In Japan, Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz uses channels 1 to 13. This lab uses **channel 11**.
- Other devices sending on the same band can add noise to your recordings. If a
  recording looks strange, write down what else was running (Wi-Fi router, microwave,
  Bluetooth) in your notes. It could become a free-research question!
- Radio rules differ between countries, which is one reason you should only use boards
  with the Japanese certification mark (技適). See
  [Safety](../../start_here/04_safety.md).
